## Part 1: Market for Lemons: How Akerlof's theory applies to gambling

The topic of information gathering and asymmetry in markets is a well 
documented area of finance, but I’ve noticed a number of contradictions 
in today’s current market and my own life. I want to discuss how markets 
don’t quite behave in the way Akerlof suggests in his paper “The Market 
for Lemons.” 

Akerlof proposes a car market with sellers offering different qualities of 
cars. Some may be run-down and in poor condition, whereas others simply 
weren’t the right fit for the owner or had to be sold when the owner moved 
across the country. To the seller, it is next to impossible to distinguish 
which car is of good quality and which is of not. From the outside, without 
additional information, the buyer has no idea of identifying the better cars. 
It seems reasonable, then, that they would be willing to buy a car for the average 
value of all cars in the market. But a sort of progression happens instantaneously. 
Higher value cars will not enter the market if buyers are offering an average price; 
that price will be below the value of the high value cars. If these high value cars exit the market, 
the average price a buyer offers will fall, until the market is only left with the lowest quality car. 
This is not good for either buyer or sellers. Majority of sellers lose the ability to sell their car at appropriate prices, 
and buyers are given only the worst quality goods. The way to fix this problem 
is simply supply information in markets. In the car world, this looks like 
test drives, a website like CarFax, or reviews on a platform like Yelp. 
To summarize, information is the key conduit to efficient markets. 

What about this all is interesting? Well, I do find it to be one of the 
most foundational principles to lay the groundwork in markets. Despite this, 
I’ve noticed a number of glaring contradictions in my own observed life that 
I find interesting. Many of these examples can be explained as well, people 
acting irrationally, but I will attempt to make sense of things nonetheless.

I dipped my toes into the gaming industry just a few weeks ago by entering a 
casino. I find myself an avid poker player, but I have trouble rationalizing 
many of the other casino offerings.

Poker eliminates the house; there is no house edge, except for the rake. I see 
myself in the worst case, as an average poker player.  In the worst case, I will 
be breaking even in a casual home game against other average players. When the rake 
gets involved, I have to be slightly better than average to break even.

That is beside the point. Where poker seems justifiable as hypothetically profitable, 
or at least a break even game, every other casino game gives the house an edge that ensures 
you will lose money in the long run. Not only that, but this edge is well-documented and no 
secret. The American Gambling Association documents the average edge for casinos across the 
country here. This is the only way they continue to operate these games. If they were unprofitable, 
they would no longer be offered, or you would be asked to stop playing. Despite this, gaming across 
America increased 2.1% in Q1 of 2026. Why does gambling continue to grow?

This phenomenon directly contradicts Akerlof’s theory. Despite being supplied information about the 
house edge, gamblers continue to pour more and more money into sports betting and casinos. Gambling 
seems evidence that while information supply enables efficient markets, it doesn’t mandate them; 
humans may disregard this information given the appropriate psychological environment. Advertising, 
gaming design, and nonlinear utility, and other psychological factors distract from gaming most important 
information. 

Individuals can make a career out of gaming, but advertising revenue and sponsorship drive income, 
not income from play. All these players are encouraged to play to improve the gambling industry's 
visibility and might even have their losses subsidized by the casino. There is little use in having 
promotional content for a losing spokesperson. Of course, these public gambling figures will appear 
to be making money! While these players appear to be making money gambling, this should not be attributed 
to superior play. Poker professionals are the exception to this rule; their player vs player winnings should 
largely be attributed to skill. These sirens for gambling fortune only attract additional customers to play. 

The psychological effects I am less familiar with. I’ll rattle of ideas of what I believe to be true, but less 
so my area of expertise. We aren’t considering the personal value of sitting down at a table of friends, and 
having a time at the casino. Maybe that is well worth the $50 spent? People don’t calculate expected value, and 
overvalue big payouts.

My perspective on gambling remains simple. I should accept gambling as a 
financial loss for some personal enjoyment or satisfaction. I can throw 
away $10 at a casino not as an investment or sound financial strategy, 
but to enjoy myself. If I want to minimize my losses, I will bet infrequently 
and bet the entire amount I wish to risk. The longer I play, the more certain 
I am to become negative EV. And also, probably just stick to poker.
