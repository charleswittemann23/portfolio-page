# Cryptocurrency Mining Practices: Mitigating Climate Change and Creating a Sustainable Crypto Future

When many people first think of cryptocurrency, several stories about Bitcoin usually dominate their interest. Bitcoin millionaires, people who bought large amounts of Bitcoin early in its lifespan, or accepted it as payment when it was obscure, have created the sentiment that Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies are the powerful currencies of the future. Bitcoin, with a secure peer-to-peer security network and prioritization of privacy, has come with a myriad of dilemmas, namely the large carbon emissions generated by its mining. By changing the Bitcoin mining procedure to incentivize climate-conscious mining, miners will substantially reduce global warming and create a more sustainable coin community for the future.

In 2008, mysterious figurehead Satoshi Nakamoto published the open-source code for the Bitcoin system, now synonymous with cryptocurrency. At a basic level, Bitcoin operates through a blockchain system, essentially a series of peer-to-peer connections and confirmations of transactions. This eliminates the need for a financial institution to confirm digital transfers of assets. Bitcoin mining, in essence, hinges on computers running an algorithm to both confirm transactions and work for additional currency (Nakamoto 1). Proponents of cryptocurrency advertise Bitcoin as the future of modern currency that takes away a central governing body of money by creating a finite, secure online system for finance. Bitcoin steadily grew in popularity in the early 2010s until the crypto frenzy took hold in 2020. The market has calmed down since then, but there is still discussion of how it will be incorporated into payment systems and how the government can regulate it. However, several issues are holding back this system. In the case of Bitcoin mining, despite being entirely electronic and digital, it deepens the poisonous relationship between mining and climate change in new ways.

The Bitcoin mining process, despite being industry-defining, has several problems concerning carbon emissions. The main issue lies not in the mining algorithm but in the massive amounts of electricity required to power Bitcoin farms. At the moment, thirty-four large cryptocurrency farms in America use enough electricity to power thirty thousand homes (J.X. Dance 1). Globally, Bitcoin electricity consumption totals twenty-two terawatts-hours annually, nearly four times the amount consumed by Google in the same span. "Bitcoin mining consumes more energy than most small countries" and makes it increasingly more difficult to enjoy "progress made in reducing global carbon emissions." (Gulli 115)

However, many large Bitcoin companies have disputed the idea of contributing to carbon emissions with a couple of technical points. Most Bitcoin farms tout their proximity to remote renewable energy sources as a sign of energy-conscious business. However, in WattTime's marginal emissions analysis, thirty-four of the biggest crypto mines in total "cause sixteen point four million tons of carbon pollution each year" (J.X. Dance 4). Renewable energy sources look helpful but practically don't sustain mining at this large scale. Additionally, miners question whether this problem pertains to them at all. In a letter to the Environmental Protection Agency, Bitcoin advocate Nic Carter notes that the associated emissions are a function of electricity generation (J.X. Dance 3). Rather than actively polluting the Earth, crypto companies just use an already flawed electrical system. This argument sounds like a lazy way to avoid blame. Sure, the problem of carbon emissions stems from decades of pollution, not just Bitcoin mining. However, the problem remains, and farms don't do enough. Large profit margins may make it easier to turn a blind eye to this problem, but the fact remains that this large electricity consumption has serious repercussions.

The solution to reducing carbon emissions from Bitcoin farms hinges on a twofold approach that should both slow down emissions and lay the foundation for more sustainable practices. Firstly, the Bitcoin algorithm responsible for generating coins should have a self-imposed annual cap on coins generated annually. At face value, this system should limit the effectiveness of large crypto farms that consume massive amounts of electricity. Less electricity will be consumed if farms have a hard cap on production for a total year. This will reduce the imminent danger that comes with large global warming effects. Miners might be potentially angry at reducing production, but this sort of measure has its advantages for the farms. A more stagnant supply means that each coin mined will be worth more because coin will be in greater demand. Additionally, a slowdown in production ensures a more long-term investment in the currency. Farms are no longer focused on producing coins in large amounts, rather they are concerned with the long-term viability of Bitcoin. Secondly, this slows down mining to give time for companies to make radical changes in the way they mine. Not only is this good for the environment, but potentially important if crypto mining is to continue in the future. An imminent concern for miners is what will happen when Bitcoin halving happens. For miners, this means that each successful "mine" will return half the original amount of bitcoin, as the system halves the rate at which bitcoin is released (Whittaker 1). This means that for farms that aren't having substantial returns, it doesn't make sense to continue farming. Putting an annual cap delays this inevitability, but gives miners a chance to become more efficient by adopting alternative energy sources. These farms already use renewable energy sources, but not at a level that significantly offsets their carbon emissions. Although expensive, sources like solar or wind, once installed, are incredibly low-cost and efficient. Once halving occurs, farms running on renewable energy will continue to mine because the low returns remain profitable.

This sort of transition seems obvious, but several challenges stand in its way. Changes to the Bitcoin structure require ninety percent of miners' approval, something that will be hard to find for such large changes. There will need to be near-unanimous support for these changes from the crypto community. Traditional Nakamoto supporters might find an issue with this solution, namely because making large changes to the Bitcoin system might dilute the potency of a strong, independent, monetary system. These concerns are warranted, which is exactly why these changes will be so hard to make. In a lot of ways, the revision of Bitcoin can be likened to the establishment of the American federal government, which is rather ironic considering the contrast between the two ideologically. In hopes of preserving Bitcoin, miners will need to make a few concessions, not unlike states conceding their individual currencies and trade agreements when signing the US Constitution. It may feel like the very thing that the United States fought against, but a strong central government, with proper checks and balances, proved to be the crown jewel of modern democracy. In a similar sense, making revisions to the system may feel counterintuitive, but ultimately, it will set Bitcoin up for a more sustainable future, potentially becoming the model for all successive cryptocurrencies.

Cryptocurrency will continue to be a powerful force in finance regardless of its effect on the climate. Bitcoin farms will continue onward simply because mining yields incredible profits. However, sustainable mining practices must be implemented in the best interest of a sustainable future. Bitcoin companies and advocates can't possibly expect the general population to invest in this future currency of the world when cryptocurrencies aren't doing enough to invest in our Earth's climate. Miners will be hurt by the initial drop in production and efficiency that comes with these changes, however, these changes are an investment into its future. By investing in renewable energy sources for mining and slowing down the mining process, Bitcoin farms have the potential to become the future of modern transaction services.
